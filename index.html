NFL player stats, 2009 to 2025: passing peaked, defenses changed

By Rykar Acebo

This report covers 291,750 regular-season player-games from the 2009 through 2025 NFL seasons, taken from the nflverse weekly player stats. It looks at seasons and games rather than careers, so the start of the data never cuts a player off. Passing yards per team-game peaked in 2015 at 259.2 and fell to 225.0 by 2025, while rushing stayed within 109 to 122. Over the same 17 seasons, interceptions per team-game fell 32% and sacks per pass attempt rose from 6.4% to 7.3%. The data show what changed, not why. A last pair of findings compares quarterbacks with a typical backup, using a stat built for this site. The dashboard lets you filter the same data, switch between player and team views, and try more advanced stats.

Finding 01 · Passing peaked, then slid

Passing yards per team-game peaked at 259.2 in 2015, topped 250 in 7 of the 8 seasons from 2013 to 2020 (all but 2017), then fell to 225.0 in 2025; rushing stayed within 109 to 122

Adding up every player's passing yards and dividing by team-games, offenses averaged 232.3 passing yards in 2009, climbed to 259.2 in 2015, and fell to 225.0 in 2025, the lowest of the 17 seasons. That is 13.2% below the peak. The path is not a smooth arc: 2017 dipped to 239.6, and every season from 2021 on was below 250.

Rushing barely moved. Rushing yards per team-game stayed between 108.8 and 121.6 every season, and finished 2025 at 116.9. In these numbers the drop is in passing; rushing did not move with it.

Finding 02 · Passing peaked, then slid

Fewer quarterbacks reach 4,000 yards: 13 did in 2016, only 6 in 2025

A 4,000-yard passing season used to be common. In 2016, 13 quarterbacks got there, the most of any season. It was 6 in both 2024 and 2025.

This counts each player's regular-season total for one season. Seasons were 16 games through 2020 and 17 from 2021, so counts from 2021 on have a little extra room to reach the threshold. Each block in the chart is one player, colored by his team; hover a block to see who.

Finding 03 · The running back roller coaster

1,000-yard rushers swing widely, from 7 in some seasons to 17 in others

The number of players who ran for 1,000 yards in a season was 17 in 2010, fell to 7 in 2015 and 7 in 2021, then came back to 16 in 2024 and 17 in 2025.

There is no steady trend to explain it. The count is lowest in 2015, 2021 (7) and highest in 2010, 2025 (17). Each block in the chart is one player, colored by his team; hover a block to see who.

Finding 04 · The running back roller coaster

300-carry seasons: 7 in 2010, never more than 3 a year from 2013 to 2023, then 6 in 2024 and 4 in 2025

A 300-carry season was more common early on: 6 players in 2009 and 7 in 2010. From 2013 to 2023 the count never passed 3, and in 2023 no one reached it.

The count was 6 in 2024 and 4 in 2025. The counts are small, so one or two bell-cow backs (a running back who gets most of his team's carries) make a visible difference. Each block in the chart is one player, colored by his team; hover a block to see who.

Finding 05 · Receivers pile up catches

100-catch seasons rose from 6 in 2009 to 12 in 2023, though team passing yards per game were 236 in 2023 against 259 at the 2015 peak

The count was 6 in 2009 and only 2 in 2010 and 2011. It reached 12 in 2023 and was 7 in 2025, so the rise is uneven.

The count of 100-catch players and team passing yards per game did not move together: 12 players in 2023 with 236.3 yards per team-game, against 7 players in 2015 with 259.2. These are counts of individual players, so a few high-volume receivers can move the number from one season to the next. The data do not say why. Each block in the chart is one player, colored by his team; hover a block to see who.

Finding 06 · Defenses: fewer takeaways, more pressure

Interceptions per team-game fell from 1.03 in 2009 to 0.70 in 2025

Defenses came up with 1.025 interceptions per team-game in 2009. By 2025 that had fallen to 0.699, a drop of 32%.

The decline is gradual, with no single-season cliff. The data show that it happened, not why.

Finding 07 · Defenses: fewer takeaways, more pressure

Players with 6+ interceptions fell from 11 in 2009 to between 1 and 5 a year since 2019

In 2009, 11 defenders had six or more interceptions. By 2013 it was 4, and in 2025 only 1 player did it. Every season from 2019 to 2025 had between 1 and 5.

The season high in the data is 11 interceptions, by Trevon Diggs in 2021. Each block in the chart is one player, colored by his team; hover a block to see who.

Finding 08 · Defenses: fewer takeaways, more pressure

Sacks per pass attempt rose from 6.4% in 2009 to 7.3% in 2025

Dividing sacks by pass attempts, the sack rate was 6.38% in 2009 and 7.33% in 2025. It peaked at 7.64% in 2023.

Sacks are not counted in pass attempts, so this rate is sacks per attempt, not per dropback (a dropback is a pass attempt or a sack). Over the same seasons interceptions per team-game fell while sacks per attempt rose. The data show what changed, not why.

Finding 09 · Defenses: fewer takeaways, more pressure

J.J. Watt owns 4 of the 10 biggest QB-hit seasons, led by 51 in 2014

The top 10 single seasons for quarterback hits include 4 by J.J. Watt, in 2012 (43), 2013 (46), 2014 (51), 2015 (50). The record in the data is 51.

The source does not say how consistently QB hits were recorded over time, so treat this as a ranking of recorded hits, not a full count of pressure.

Finding 10 · Defenses: fewer takeaways, more pressure

The sack leader each season ranged from 15 (T.J. Watt, 2020) to 23 (Myles Garrett, 2025)

Every season has one clear sack leader, with no ties. The lowest leading total was 15 by T.J. Watt in 2020. The highest was 23 by Myles Garrett in 2025.

T.J. Watt led the league in 3 of the 17 seasons. Each bar is colored by the leader's team, and a leader who changed teams that season would show a bar split between them. Hover a bar to see who led.

Finding 11 · Value over replacement

By expected points over replacement, Patrick Mahomes in 2022 was the best quarterback season, and only 4 of the top 10 also top the passing-yards list

EPA (expected points added) says how many points a play was worth compared with the average play in the same situation. Made-up round numbers as an example: if a drive is worth 1.0 expected points before a pass and 2.0 after a 20-yard completion, that pass has an EPA of +1.0; an interception that drops the drive's value to -1.0 has an EPA of -2.0. EPA over replacement (EPAOR) is a quarterback's total EPA minus what a replacement-level passer (a typical backup, defined in finding 12) would have added on the same number of opportunities (his pass attempts, sacks and carries). The top season in 2009 to 2025 is Patrick Mahomes in 2022 with 308.1 points, ranked 4 in passing yards across all 17 seasons.

Ranking all quarterback seasons by EPAOR and by passing yards, 4 of the top 10 seasons appear on both lists. Passing yards reward volume; EPAOR also rewards efficiency and counts sacks, interceptions and scrambles. This is this site's own version of a value-over-replacement stat, not a full WAR. The replacement rule is in the data section below.

Finding 12 · Value over replacement

Under this site's rule, quarterback replacement level averaged -0.113 EPA per play across the 17 seasons, below zero and below the 0.064 of the top 32

Each season, the quarterbacks ranked below the top 32 by plays form the replacement pool. Their EPA per play averaged -0.113, from -0.187 (2009) to -0.067 (2020). The top 32 averaged 0.064, above the replacement level in 17 of 17 seasons.

Read these figures with care. The pool is biased low by construction: it holds the players who ended up with the fewest plays, which includes benched and injured players, so it is a low bar and not a typical backup. The -0.113 is a simple mean of 17 season rates, not one rate pooled over every play, and the range from -0.187 to -0.067 is just the lowest and highest season, not a trend. The rate is noisy because the pool is made of players with few plays.

For wide receivers the same rule gives a replacement level that averaged 0.125 EPA per opportunity (carries plus targets), from 0.036 (2017) to 0.219 (2020), against 0.241 for the top 64. The last two columns of the table show it by season. The bar differs by position, so EPAOR scores should only be compared within a position.

About the data

Where the numbers come from and how they are computed

Source. The nflverse-data stats_player release: weekly player stats, one file per season, licensed CC-BY-4.0. Credit to the nflverse project and its contributors. Definitions of the advanced columns come from the nflverse player stats data dictionary.

What one row is. One row is one player in one game. The cleaned data has 304,515 rows and 69 columns for 17 seasons (2009 to 2025), 8,093 players and 32 teams. The time column is season (and week) and the group columns are player and team. This report and the dashboard's starting view use regular-season rows only, which is 291,750 of them; playoffs can be added with the dashboard's season-type filter.

Rows dropped and other cleaning.

  • 362 rows dropped for having no player. These rows have no player ID, no player name and no position, so they cannot be a player-game. There is exactly one such row for each of the 362 season-weeks in the source (21 to 22 per season, 294 in the regular season and 68 in the playoffs), not one per team per week. Each has a team code, and 337 of them have non-zero stats, almost all penalties (330 rows) and safeties (84 rows). The source does not say what they are; they look like penalties and other plays charged to a team instead of a player. What was lost, counting regular-season totals in the dropped rows out of all rows: sacks 11 of 20,708, interceptions 0 of 7,521, solo tackles 163 of 353,204, QB hits 6 of 47,631, passing yards 0 of 2,163,026, rushing yards 0 of 1,016,067, receptions 2 of 191,313. Penalties are the one number that is visibly affected: 2,764 of 55,658 (5.0%) sit in dropped rows, so penalty rates on the dashboard and the team field are about 5% lower than the source's total. Nothing in the findings above uses penalties.
  • 4,536 all-zero rows kept. 4,536 rows have a player ID, name, position and team but no tracked stat in the columns kept here (mostly linemen, defensive backs and linebackers who played without recording a stat). They are real players who appeared in the game (1.5% of regular-season rows), so dropping them would understate who played. They add nothing to any sum, and they do count as player-games in the "Player-games" measure and in per-player-game averages.
  • Team codes. The source uses one code per franchise across moves: the Raiders are LV, the Chargers LAC and the Rams LA in every season, so there are 32 teams in all 17 seasons and no renaming was needed.
  • Loaded 304,877 player-game rows from 2009 to 2025 (17 seasons)
  • Dropped 0 rows with no opponent team
  • Dropped 0 duplicate player-game rows
  • Column def_tackles_for_loss has no non-zero value in 2009: written as NA (not recorded that season), not 0
  • Column def_tackles_for_loss has no non-zero value in 2010: written as NA (not recorded that season), not 0
  • Column def_tackles_for_loss has no non-zero value in 2011: written as NA (not recorded that season), not 0

Tackles for loss are not recorded in 2009 to 2011 and are marked NA (not zero) there. More generally, NA means the source has no value for that player in that game (for example, no pass attempts means no passing EPA); sums skip NA. Player names are not unique, so players are identified by player_id. Full details are in data/DATA_QUALITY.md and data/DATA_DICTIONARY.md.

Terms in plain words.

EPA (expected points added)
How many points a play was worth compared with the average play in the same situation. Example with made-up round numbers: a drive worth 1.0 expected points before a pass and 2.0 after a 20-yard catch gives that pass an EPA of +1.0. The values come from nflverse's model, not from this project.
Dropback
A pass attempt or a sack: every time the quarterback drops back to pass. EPA per dropback is passing EPA divided by attempts plus sacks.
CPOE (completion percentage over expected)
How much more or less often a passer completes passes than nflverse's model expects given how hard each throw was, in percentage points. +2 means two points better than expected.
Opportunities
The plays a player was directly involved in: pass attempts + sacks + carries for a quarterback, carries + targets for a running back, receiver or tight end.
Replacement level
What the players just below the starters produced: this site's rule puts everyone ranked below the top 32 quarterbacks (64 receivers) by opportunities in the replacement pool. The pool includes benched and injured players, so it is a low bar.
EPA over replacement (EPAOR)
A player's total EPA minus what the replacement rate would have produced on the same number of opportunities.
Bell-cow
A running back who gets most of his team's carries.

How every number is computed.

  • Team-game: one team in one game. Per-team-game rates add up every player's stat for the season and divide by the number of regular-season team-games (32 teams, 16 games each through 2020, 17 from 2021, with one fewer game in 2022).
  • Player-season counts (4,000-yard passers, 1,000-yard rushers, 300-carry backs, 100-catch receivers, players with 6+ interceptions): each player's regular-season total for one season is compared to the threshold, and players who met it are counted once. A player who changed teams has one total across teams.
  • Interceptions per team-game is defensive interceptions divided by team-games.
  • Sacks per pass attempt is defensive sacks (halves count) divided by pass attempts. Sacks are not part of attempts.
  • QB hits and sack leaders are single-season player totals. Ranking ties are broken by player name. No two players tied for the sack lead in any season.
  • Advanced stats on the dashboard: EPA per dropback is passing EPA divided by attempts plus sacks. ANY/A is (passing yards + 20 x touchdowns - 45 x interceptions + sack yards lost) divided by (attempts + sacks), with sack yards stored as negatives. CPOE is each game's CPOE weighted by that game's attempts. RACR is receiving yards divided by receiving air yards. Target share, air-yards share and WOPR are averages over games in which the player was targeted. Each dashboard measure states its definition under the chart.
  • EPA over replacement (EPAOR, this site's version): regular season only, one position group at a time (quarterbacks, running backs, wide receivers, tight ends). Opportunities are attempts + sacks + carries for quarterbacks and carries + targets for the others. Each season players are ranked by opportunities (ties by player ID); the top 32 quarterbacks, 32 running backs, 64 wide receivers and 32 tight ends are the starters and everyone below them is the replacement pool. The replacement rate r is the pool's total EPA divided by the pool's opportunities, and EPAOR = a player's EPA - r x opportunities. The cutoffs are a judgment call, not nflWAR's. Scores are in expected points, not wins, and are only comparable within a position. A pass's EPA appears under both the passer and the receiver, so EPA must not be added across players.

Limits. Stats are only as good as the source; tackles depend on stat-crew scoring and are not used here. Metrics that need play-by-play, Next Gen Stats or Pro Football Reference data (EPA allowed, pressure rate, time to throw, missed tackles) are not in this dataset. Reasons for the trends, such as rule changes, are outside the data and are not tested here.

Every number on this page is computed by scripts/build_report.py from data/seasons/, and scripts/check_numbers.js recomputes them with the dashboard's own code.
